This chapter sketches the history of randomized computation and Monte Carlo counting. It begins with Valiant's counting classes and Toda's theorem, continues with the early randomized algorithms (the Metropolis algorithm, the Solovay and Strassen and Rabin primality tests, Gill's definition of RP, BPP, ZPP and PP, polynomial identity testing and the Schwartz and Zippel lemma), and then the hardness versus randomness program (Yao, Blum and Micali, Nisan and Wigderson, Impagliazzo and Wigderson) and Impagliazzo's five worlds. A final part traces Monte Carlo counting: Knuth's estimator for backtrack trees, splitting (Kahn and Harris), Jerrum, Valiant and Vazirani on counting and sampling, annealed importance sampling, nested sampling, and the observation of Polson and Scott that every ladder of distributions traverses the quantile function of the likelihood. It is a survey and presents no new theorem.

## From the literature

Valiant's #P and the permanent (1979); Toda's theorem (1991); Metropolis algorithm (1953); Solovay and Strassen (1977) and Rabin (1980) primality tests, Miller's test (1976); Gill's probabilistic classes (1977); Schwartz and Zippel lemma (1979, 1980); Yao (1982), Blum and Micali (1982); Nisan and Wigderson (1994); Impagliazzo and Wigderson (1997); Impagliazzo's five worlds (1995); Knuth on tree size estimation (1975); Jerrum, Valiant and Vazirani (1986); Skilling's nested sampling (2006); Polson and Scott on vertical likelihood (2015).
