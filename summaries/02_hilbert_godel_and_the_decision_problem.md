This chapter traces the foundations of computation that lead to the P versus NP question. It starts with Hilbert's program (completeness, consistency and decidability of mathematics) and his tenth problem, presents Gödel's incompleteness theorems and the idea of arithmetising syntax, and asks what a mechanical procedure is. It compares the three formalisations of the 1930s, recursive functions, Church's lambda calculus and Turing machines, states the Church Turing thesis, and explains how Church and Turing showed that the Entscheidungsproblem has no solution, via the halting problem and diagonalisation. It reports Matiyasevich's solution of the tenth problem. It closes with the step from computability to complexity: the bounded version of the Entscheidungsproblem (does a formula have a proof of length at most n) is in NP and NP complete, which is the question in Gödel's 1956 letter. The chapter proves nothing new and fixes the historical and logical vocabulary used in the rest of Part I.

## From the literature

Hilbert's program and tenth problem (1900, 1920s); Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems (1931); Gödel and Herbrand recursive functions with Kleene; Church's lambda calculus and undecidability of first order provability (1936); Turing's machines, universal machine and halting problem (1936); Matiyasevich's theorem with Davis, Putnam and Julia Robinson (1970); Hartmanis and Stearns on time complexity (1960s); Gödel's letter to von Neumann (1956).
